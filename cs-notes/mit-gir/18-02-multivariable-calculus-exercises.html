Calculus II (18.02) - Exercises

Vectors and Matrices

Product

Use dot product for length, angle, and projection; use cross product for normal vectors, area, and orientation.

Angle and area

Given

\vec A=\langle 1,2,-1\rangle,\qquad \vec B=\langle 2,0,3\rangle,

find the angle between them and the area of the parallelogram they span.

\vec A\cdot \vec B=1\cdot 2+2\cdot 0+(-1)\cdot 3=-1

|\vec A|=\sqrt6,\qquad |\vec B|=\sqrt{13}

So

\cos\theta=\frac{\vec A\cdot\vec B}{|\vec A||\vec B|}=-\frac1{\sqrt{78}}.

For the area,

\vec A\times\vec B= \begin{vmatrix} \hat i & \hat j & \hat k \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 & 3 \end{vmatrix} =\langle 6,-5,-4\rangle.

Thus

|\vec A\times\vec B|=\sqrt{36+25+16}=\sqrt{77}.

Projection

Find the projection of \vec A=\langle 3,4,0\rangle onto \vec B=\langle 1,2,2\rangle.

The scalar projection is

\operatorname{comp}_{\vec B}\vec A=\frac{\vec A\cdot\vec B}{|\vec B|} =\frac{3+8+0}{3}=\frac{11}{3}.

The vector projection is

\operatorname{proj}_{\vec B}\vec A =\frac{\vec A\cdot\vec B}{|\vec B|^2}\vec B =\frac{11}{9}\langle 1,2,2\rangle.

Normal vector

Find a normal vector to the plane through

P=(1,0,0),\qquad Q=(0,1,0),\qquad R=(0,0,1).

Use two vectors in the plane:

\overrightarrow{PQ}=\langle -1,1,0\rangle, \qquad \overrightarrow{PR}=\langle -1,0,1\rangle.

Then

\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR} =\langle 1,1,1\rangle.

So one normal vector is

\langle 1,1,1\rangle.

If a problem asks for angle, try \vec A\cdot\vec B; if it asks for area or a normal vector, try \vec A\times\vec B.

Determinant

Use a 2\times2 determinant for signed area in the plane, and a scalar triple product for signed volume in space.

Find the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by

\vec A=\langle 1,0,2\rangle,\qquad \vec B=\langle 2,1,0\rangle,\qquad \vec C=\langle 0,3,1\rangle.

Compute

\vec B\times\vec C= \begin{vmatrix} \hat i & \hat j & \hat k \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} =\langle 1,-2,6\rangle.

Then

\vec A\cdot(\vec B\times\vec C)=\langle 1,0,2\rangle\cdot\langle 1,-2,6\rangle=13.

So the volume is

|\vec A\cdot(\vec B\times\vec C)|=13.

The sign records orientation; area and volume use the absolute value.

Inverse Matrix

To solve a small linear system, either row-reduce or use the inverse matrix when the determinant is nonzero.

Solve

\begin{cases} 2x+y=5,\\ x+3y=7. \end{cases}

Write

A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad \vec b=\begin{pmatrix}5\\7\end{pmatrix}.

Since

\det A=2\cdot3-1\cdot1=5\neq0,

A^{-1}=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.

Thus

\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} =A^{-1}\vec b =\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}5\\7\end{pmatrix} =\frac15\begin{pmatrix}8\\9\end{pmatrix}.

So

x=\frac85, \qquad y=\frac95.

A zero determinant means the inverse method is unavailable; expect no solution or infinitely many solutions.

Partial Derivatives

Partial Derivatives

When taking f_x, treat y,z,\ldots as constants; when taking f_y, treat x,z,\ldots as constants.

For

f(x,y)=x^2y+e^{xy}+\sin y,

f_x=2xy+ye^{xy}.

At (1,0),

f_x(1,0)=0.

For the other partial derivative,

f_y=x^2+xe^{xy}+\cos y.

At (1,0),

f_y(1,0)=1+1+1=3.

Partial derivatives are ordinary one-variable derivatives after deciding which variables are frozen.

Least Square

Convert “best fit” into minimizing squared error, then set partial derivatives to zero.

Fit y=ax+b through points (0,1),(1,2),(2,2) in the least-squares sense.

The error is

D(a,b)=(1-b)^2+(2-a-b)^2+(2-2a-b)^2.

Set

\frac{\partial D}{\partial a}=0, \qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial b}=0.

This gives

\begin{cases} 5a+3b=6,\\ 3a+3b=5. \end{cases}

Solving,

a=\frac12, \qquad b=\frac76.

So the fitted line is

y=\frac12x+\frac76.

Least squares is an optimization problem in the unknown parameters, not in the data variables.

Min/Max

Find critical points first, then classify them by the second derivative test.

For

f(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2-3x-3y,

critical points satisfy

f_x=2x+y-3=0, \qquad f_y=x+2y-3=0.

Solving gives

x=1, \qquad y=1.

Now

f_{xx}=2, \qquad f_{xy}=1, \qquad f_{yy}=2.

So

AC-B^2=2\cdot2-1^2=3>0, \qquad A=2>0.

Thus (1,1) is a local minimum.

The second derivative test only classifies critical points; it does not find them.

Taylor’s Theorem

Around a point, approximate a multivariable function using value, gradient, and second derivatives.

Find the second-order Taylor polynomial of

f(x,y)=e^{x+y}

at (0,0).

Since every partial derivative of e^{x+y} is e^{x+y}, all needed derivatives at (0,0) are 1.

Thus

T_2(x,y)=1+x+y+\frac12x^2+xy+\frac12y^2.

The mixed term xy comes from f_{xy}xy, not from \frac12 f_{xy}xy twice forgotten; the full quadratic form is

\frac12(f_{xx}x^2+2f_{xy}xy+f_{yy}y^2).

Total Differential

Use total differential to estimate small changes.

For

f(x,y,z)=x^2y+z^3,

df=2xy\,dx+x^2\,dy+3z^2\,dz.

At (1,2,1), with

dx=0.01, \qquad dy=-0.02, \qquad dz=0.03,

df\approx 4(0.01)+1(-0.02)+3(0.03)=0.11.

df is a linear approximation; it is most useful when all changes are small.

Chain Rule

Draw the dependency tree, then multiply derivatives along each path and add paths that end at the same variable.

Dependency tree

Let

w=x^2+y^2, \qquad x=s+t, \qquad y=st.

Find \partial w/\partial s.

By chain rule,

\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=w_xx_s+w_yy_s.

Since

w_x=2x, \qquad w_y=2y, \qquad x_s=1, \qquad y_s=t,

\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=2x+2yt.

Substitute x=s+t and y=st:

\frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=2(s+t)+2st^2.

Polar parameters

Let

z=e^{xy}, \qquad x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta.

Find \partial z/\partial r.

By chain rule,

\frac{\partial z}{\partial r}=z_xx_r+z_yy_r.

Since

z_x=ye^{xy}, \qquad z_y=xe^{xy}, \qquad x_r=\cos\theta, \qquad y_r=\sin\theta,

\frac{\partial z}{\partial r} =e^{xy}(y\cos\theta+x\sin\theta).

Substitute x=r\cos\theta and y=r\sin\theta:

\frac{\partial z}{\partial r} =2r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,e^{r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}.

Single-variable parameter

Let

w=xy+yz+zx, \qquad x=t, \qquad y=t^2, \qquad z=e^t.

Find dw/dt at t=0.

Use

\frac{dw}{dt}=w_x\frac{dx}{dt}+w_y\frac{dy}{dt}+w_z\frac{dz}{dt}.

Here

w_x=y+z, \qquad w_y=x+z, \qquad w_z=x+y.

At t=0,

x=0, \qquad y=0, \qquad z=1,

and

\frac{dx}{dt}=1, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=2t=0, \qquad \frac{dz}{dt}=e^t=1.

Thus

\frac{dw}{dt}=1\cdot1+1\cdot0+0\cdot1=1.

Differentiate first in terms of intermediate variables, then substitute if the answer should use the original variables.

Gradient Vector

The gradient is normal to level curves and level surfaces.

Normal vector

For

w=x^2+y^2+z^2,

the level surface w=9 is a sphere. At P=(1,2,2),

\nabla w=\langle 2x,2y,2z\rangle=\langle 2,4,4\rangle.

So a normal vector to the sphere at P is

\langle 2,4,4\rangle.

Intersection tangent

Find a tangent vector to the curve where

F(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2=6

and

G(x,y,z)=x+y+z=4

intersect at P=(1,1,2).

The curve lies on both level surfaces, so its tangent vector is perpendicular to both gradients:

\nabla F(P)=\langle 2,2,4\rangle, \qquad \nabla G(P)=\langle 1,1,1\rangle.

Thus one tangent vector is

\nabla F(P)\times\nabla G(P) =\begin{vmatrix} \hat i & \hat j & \hat k \\ 2 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} =\langle -2,2,0\rangle.

So a simpler tangent vector is

\langle -1,1,0\rangle.

Tangent line

Find the tangent line to the level curve

F(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2=7

at P=(1,2).

The gradient is

\nabla F=\langle 2x+y,x+2y\rangle.

At P,

\nabla F(1,2)=\langle 4,5\rangle.

The tangent line is perpendicular to the gradient, so

\langle 4,5\rangle\cdot\langle x-1,y-2\rangle=0.

Thus

4x+5y=14.

For an implicit surface w(x,y,z)=c, use \nabla w as a normal vector.

Rate of Change in an Arbitrary Direction

Normalize the direction vector before taking the dot product with the gradient.

For

f(x,y)=x^2y+y^3,

find the directional derivative at (1,2) in direction \vec v=\langle 3,4\rangle.

First

\nabla f=\langle 2xy,x^2+3y^2\rangle.

At (1,2),

\nabla f(1,2)=\langle 4,13\rangle.

Normalize the direction:

\hat u=\frac{\langle 3,4\rangle}{5}=\left\langle \frac35,\frac45\right\rangle.

Then

D_{\hat u}f=\nabla f\cdot\hat u =4\cdot\frac35+13\cdot\frac45 =\frac{64}{5}.

The maximum directional derivative is |\nabla f|, in the direction of \nabla f.

Lagrange Multipliers

Use Lagrange multipliers for extrema with equality constraints.

Maximize

f(x,y)=xy

subject to

x^2+y^2=1.

Set

\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g, \qquad g=x^2+y^2.

So

y=2\lambda x, \qquad x=2\lambda y, \qquad x^2+y^2=1.

If x,y\neq0, multiplying the first two equations gives

xy=4\lambda^2xy \implies \lambda=\pm\frac12.

Then y=\pm x. With the constraint,

x=\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}, \qquad y=\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}.

The maximum is

\frac12,

when x and y have the same sign.

After solving the Lagrange equations, evaluate f at all candidates; the equations alone do not say which point is max or min.

Partial Derivative with Non-Independent Variables

If variables are constrained, use the differential of the constraint to eliminate the variable that is allowed to change implicitly.

Let

f(x,y,z)=xz+y, \qquad g(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2=1.

Find

\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)_y

at (x,y,z)=(\frac{\sqrt3}{2},0,\frac12).

Since y is held constant,

dy=0.

From the constraint,

dg=2x\,dx+2y\,dy+2z\,dz=0.

So

dx=-\frac zx\,dz.

Now

df=z\,dx+x\,dz+dy.

With dy=0,

df=\left(x-\frac{z^2}{x}\right)dz.

Therefore

\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)_y =x-\frac{z^2}{x}.

At the given point,

x-\frac{z^2}{x} =\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac{1/4}{\sqrt3/2} =\frac1{\sqrt3}.

The subscript in \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)_y tells which differential should be set to zero.

Double Integral in Plane

Double Integral

Choose an order of integration that describes the region with simple bounds, then integrate one variable at a time.

Find the volume below

z=x+2y

over the rectangle

R=\{(x,y):0\le x\le 2,\ 0\le y\le 1\}.

Set up the double integral:

\iint_R (x+2y)\mathrm dA =\int_0^2\int_0^1 (x+2y)\mathrm dy\mathrm dx.

Compute the inner integral first:

\int_0^1 (x+2y)\mathrm dy=x+y^2\bigg|_0^1=x+1.

Then

\int_0^2 (x+1)\mathrm dx =\left(\frac{x^2}{2}+x\right)_0^2=4.

For rectangular bounds, the inner integral treats the outer variable as a constant.

Polar Coordinates

Use polar coordinates when the region or integrand is naturally expressed by r and \theta.

Evaluate

\iint_D (x^2+y^2)\mathrm dA

where D is the disk x^2+y^2\le 4.

In polar coordinates,

x^2+y^2=r^2, \qquad \mathrm dA=r\mathrm dr\mathrm d\theta.

The disk is

0\le r\le 2, \qquad 0\le \theta\le 2\pi.

Thus

\iint_D (x^2+y^2)\mathrm dA =\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 r^3\mathrm dr\mathrm d\theta =2\pi\cdot\frac{2^4}{4}=8\pi.

The factor r in \mathrm dA=r\mathrm dr\mathrm d\theta is part of the area element, not part of the original integrand.

Area of Region

Area is the double integral of 1 over the region.

Find the area between

y=x^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad y=x

for 0\le x\le 1.

The region is

0\le x\le 1, \qquad x^2\le y\le x.

So

\operatorname{Area}(R) =\iint_R 1\mathrm dA =\int_0^1\int_{x^2}^x 1\mathrm dy\mathrm dx.

Compute

\int_0^1 (x-x^2)\mathrm dx =\left(\frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{x^3}{3}\right)_0^1 =\frac16.

Describing the top curve minus the bottom curve is the same as integrating 1 over vertical slices.

Center of Mass

For constant density, divide the first moments by the area.

Find the center of mass of the triangular lamina

R=\{(x,y):0\le x\le 1,\ 0\le y\le x\}

with constant density.

The area is

A=\int_0^1\int_0^x 1\mathrm dy\mathrm dx =\int_0^1 x\mathrm dx=\frac12.

The x-coordinate is

\overline x=\frac1A\int_0^1\int_0^x x\mathrm dy\mathrm dx =2\int_0^1 x^2\mathrm dx=\frac23.

The y-coordinate is

\overline y=\frac1A\int_0^1\int_0^x y\mathrm dy\mathrm dx =2\int_0^1 \frac{x^2}{2}\mathrm dx=\frac13.

Thus

(\overline x,\overline y)=\left(\frac23,\frac13\right).

For constant density, density cancels from the quotient, but the area in the denominator still matters.

Rotational Moment of Inertia

Square the distance to the axis, then integrate it against density.

Find the moment of inertia about the x-axis for the rectangle

R=\{(x,y):0\le x\le 2,\ 0\le y\le 1\}

with density \delta=1.

Distance to the x-axis is |y|, so

I_x=\iint_R y^2\mathrm dA =\int_0^2\int_0^1 y^2\mathrm dy\mathrm dx.

Compute

\int_0^2\left(\frac{y^3}{3}\bigg|_0^1\right)\mathrm dx =\int_0^2 \frac13\mathrm dx =\frac23.

The axis determines the distance term: about the x-axis use y^2, about the y-axis use x^2.

Jacobian Matrix

Build the Jacobian by differentiating each output variable with respect to each input variable in order.

For

u=x+y, \qquad v=x-y,

find

\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}.

The Jacobian matrix is

\mathbf J =\begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.

Therefore

\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)} =\det\mathbf J =-2.

The sign records orientation; area scaling uses the absolute value.

Change of Variables

Convert the region and multiply by the absolute Jacobian determinant for the area element.

Let

x=u+v, \qquad y=u-v,

where

0\le u\le 1, \qquad 0\le v\le 2.

Find the area of the image region in the xy-plane.

Compute

\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} =\det\begin{pmatrix} x_u & x_v \\ y_u & y_v \end{pmatrix} =\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} =-2.

Thus

\mathrm dA=\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\mathrm du\mathrm dv =2\mathrm du\mathrm dv.

The area is

\int_0^1\int_0^2 2\mathrm dv\mathrm du=4.

In a change of variables, use the Jacobian in the direction from new variables to old coordinates for \mathrm dA.

Line Integral in Plane

Vector Fields

Read a vector field by evaluating its components at the point where the arrow is based.

For

\vec F(x,y)=y\hat i+x\hat j,

find the vector at (1,2).

Substitute the point into each component:

\vec F(1,2)=2\hat i+1\hat j.

So the arrow based at (1,2) points in direction

\langle 2,1\rangle.

The point (x,y) tells where the vector is attached; \vec F(x,y) tells which vector is attached there.

Line Integral

Parametrize the curve, substitute into M\mathrm dx+N\mathrm dy, and integrate over the parameter interval.

Evaluate

\int_C y\mathrm dx+x\mathrm dy

where

C:\vec r(t)=\langle t,t^2\rangle, \qquad 0\le t\le 1.

Along the curve,

x=t, \qquad y=t^2, \qquad \mathrm dx=\mathrm dt, \qquad \mathrm dy=2t\mathrm dt.

Therefore

\int_C y\mathrm dx+x\mathrm dy =\int_0^1 (t^2\mathrm dt+t\cdot2t\mathrm dt) =\int_0^1 3t^2\mathrm dt=1.

A line integral depends on both the field and the path, so convert every piece to the same parameter.

Gradient Field

First check whether the field is a gradient field; if \vec F=\nabla f, replace the line integral by the change in the potential function.

Let

\vec F=2xy\hat i+(x^2+2y)\hat j.

Find

\int_C \vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r

from (0,0) to (1,1).

First check whether \vec F can be a gradient field. Here

M=2xy, \qquad N=x^2+2y.

Since

M_y=2x, \qquad N_x=2x,

and the field is defined on all of \mathbb R^2, the condition M_y=N_x shows that \vec F is a gradient field.

Find a potential function:

f_x=2xy \implies f=x^2y+g(y).

Then

f_y=x^2+g'(y)=x^2+2y \implies g(y)=y^2.

So

f(x,y)=x^2y+y^2.

By the fundamental theorem for gradient fields,

\int_C \vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r =f(1,1)-f(0,0)=2.

Once a potential function exists on the region, the path no longer matters, only the endpoints.

Curl

In the plane, interpret curl using circulation around a shrinking positively oriented boundary, and compute it directly as N_x-M_y for \vec F=M\hat i+N\hat j.

Local circulation density

Let

\vec F=-y\hat i+x\hat j

and let C_\varepsilon=\partial R_\varepsilon be the positively oriented boundary of the disk R_\varepsilon=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2\leq\varepsilon^2\}. Find the circulation per unit area around the origin.

Parameterize the boundary by

\vec r(t)=\varepsilon\cos t\,\hat i+\varepsilon\sin t\,\hat j, \qquad 0\leq t\leq2\pi

Then

\begin{aligned} \vec F(\vec r(t)) &=-\varepsilon\sin t\,\hat i+\varepsilon\cos t\,\hat j \\ \mathrm d\vec r &=(-\varepsilon\sin t\,\hat i+\varepsilon\cos t\,\hat j)\mathrm dt \end{aligned}

so the circulation is

\oint_{C_\varepsilon}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r =\int_0^{2\pi}\varepsilon^2\mathrm dt =2\pi\varepsilon^2

Since \operatorname{area}(R_\varepsilon)=\pi\varepsilon^2,

\frac{1}{\operatorname{area}(R_\varepsilon)} \oint_{C_\varepsilon}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r =2

This ratio is independent of \varepsilon, so its limit as \varepsilon\to0 is also 2.

The derivative formula gives the same result:

N_x-M_y=1-(-1)=2

The positive sign corresponds to counterclockwise local circulation.

Partial-derivative formula

Find the curl of

\vec F=x^2y\hat i+xy^2\hat j

Here

M=x^2y, \qquad N=xy^2

So

N_x=y^2, \qquad M_y=x^2

Therefore

\operatorname{curl}\vec F=N_x-M_y=y^2-x^2

At (1,2),

\operatorname{curl}\vec F=4-1=3

For work form M\mathrm dx+N\mathrm dy, Green’s theorem uses N_x-M_y.

Green’s Theorem

Convert a positively oriented closed line integral for work into a double integral of curl over the enclosed region.

Simple region proof

Prove Green’s theorem for a region that is both vertically and horizontally simple:

R=\{(x,y):a\le x\le b,\ g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\} =\{(x,y):c\le y\le d,\ h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y)\}.

Let C=\partial R be positively oriented. First look at the M\mathrm dx part. On vertical sides, \mathrm dx=0. The bottom boundary is traversed from left to right, and the top boundary is traversed from right to left, so

\begin{aligned} \oint_C M\mathrm dx &=\int_a^b M(x,g_1(x))\mathrm dx-\int_a^b M(x,g_2(x))\mathrm dx \\ &=-\int_a^b\bigl(M(x,g_2(x))-M(x,g_1(x))\bigr)\mathrm dx \\ &=-\int_a^b\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}M_y(x,y)\mathrm dy\mathrm dx \\ &=\iint_R -M_y\mathrm dA. \end{aligned}

Now look at the N\mathrm dy part. On horizontal sides, \mathrm dy=0. The right boundary is traversed upward, and the left boundary is traversed downward, so

\begin{aligned} \oint_C N\mathrm dy &=\int_c^d N(h_2(y),y)\mathrm dy-\int_c^d N(h_1(y),y)\mathrm dy \\ &=\int_c^d\bigl(N(h_2(y),y)-N(h_1(y),y)\bigr)\mathrm dy \\ &=\int_c^d\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)}N_x(x,y)\mathrm dx\mathrm dy \\ &=\iint_R N_x\mathrm dA. \end{aligned}

Adding the two identities gives

\oint_C M\mathrm dx+N\mathrm dy =\iint_R (N_x-M_y)\mathrm dA.

For more general regions, split the region into simple pieces; the line integrals on shared internal boundaries cancel.

Unknown boundary area

Let C be any positively oriented simple closed curve enclosing area 7. Evaluate

\oint_C (e^{x^2}-y)\mathrm dx+(x+\sin(y^2))\mathrm dy.

Direct parametrization is not available because the curve is not specified. Green’s theorem only needs the curl:

M=e^{x^2}-y, \qquad N=x+\sin(y^2).

Thus

N_x=1, \qquad M_y=-1,

so

\oint_C M\mathrm dx+N\mathrm dy =\iint_R (N_x-M_y)\mathrm dA =\iint_R 2\mathrm dA =14.

Annulus orientation

Let R be the annulus

1\le x^2+y^2\le 4.

Its positively oriented boundary is

\partial R=C_2-C_1,

where C_2 is the circle x^2+y^2=4 oriented counterclockwise, and C_1 is the circle x^2+y^2=1 oriented counterclockwise. Equivalently, the actual inner boundary of R is traversed clockwise.

Evaluate

\oint_{\partial R}-y\mathrm dx+x\mathrm dy

in two ways.

Using Green’s theorem on the annulus,

M=-y, \qquad N=x, \qquad N_x-M_y=2.

Thus

\oint_{\partial R}-y\mathrm dx+x\mathrm dy =\iint_R2\mathrm dA =2\pi(2^2-1^2) =6\pi.

Now compute the two boundary pieces directly. On C_2,

x=2\cos t, \qquad y=2\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi,

so

\int_{C_2}-y\mathrm dx+x\mathrm dy =\int_0^{2\pi}4\mathrm dt =8\pi.

For the inner boundary of the annulus, use clockwise orientation:

x=\cos t, \qquad y=-\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le2\pi.

Then

\int_{\text{inner boundary}}-y\mathrm dx+x\mathrm dy =\int_0^{2\pi}-1\mathrm dt =-2\pi.

Adding the positively oriented boundary components gives

8\pi-2\pi=6\pi.

The same result can be written as

\oint_{\partial R}=\oint_{C_2}-\oint_{C_1},

where both C_2 and C_1 are counterclockwise.

Planimeter

A planimeter measures area by tracing only the boundary of a region. The Green’s theorem idea is to choose a vector field whose curl is 1:

\vec F=-\frac y2\hat i+\frac x2\hat j.

Then

curl(\vec F)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac x2\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(-\frac y2\right)=1.

For a positively oriented boundary C=\partial R,

\operatorname{Area}(R) =\iint_R1\mathrm dA =\oint_C\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r =\frac12\oint_C x\mathrm dy-y\mathrm dx.

So the device does not need to know the inside of the shape. It only needs a mechanical way to accumulate the boundary integral \frac12\oint_C x\mathrm dy-y\mathrm dx while the tracer moves around C.

In a polar planimeter, the tracer point follows the curve, and a linkage constrains a measuring wheel. The wheel rolls only by the component of motion in its rolling direction; sideways slipping does not count. The linkage geometry makes the wheel’s accumulated rolling distance proportional to the line integral above. After calibration, the wheel reading is converted directly to area.

A digital implementation uses the same theorem with a polygon approximation. If the sampled boundary points are

(x_0,y_0),(x_1,y_1),\ldots,(x_{n-1},y_{n-1}),

and (x_n,y_n)=(x_0,y_0), then

\operatorname{Area}(R) \approx\frac12\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(x_i y_{i+1}-x_{i+1}y_i).

Counterclockwise tracing gives positive area; clockwise tracing gives negative signed area. If the coordinates are in pixels, multiply by the area scale for one pixel cell.

Green’s theorem turns boundary information into area information; in some problems, it removes the need to know the boundary parametrization at all.

Flux

For a counterclockwise parametrized curve, use \hat n\mathrm ds=\langle \mathrm dy,-\mathrm dx\rangle for outward flux.

Compute the outward flux of

\vec F=x\hat i+y\hat j

across the unit circle C oriented counterclockwise.

Use

\int_C \vec F\cdot\hat n\mathrm ds =\int_C -N\mathrm dx+M\mathrm dy.

Here M=x and N=y. Parametrize

x=\cos t, \qquad y=\sin t, \qquad 0\le t\le 2\pi.

Then

\mathrm dx=-\sin t\mathrm dt, \qquad \mathrm dy=\cos t\mathrm dt.

Thus

\int_C -y\mathrm dx+x\mathrm dy =\int_0^{2\pi}(\sin^2t+\cos^2t)\mathrm dt =2\pi.

Work uses the tangent direction; flux uses the normal direction.

Divergence

In the plane, divergence is M_x+N_y for \vec F=M\hat i+N\hat j.

Find the divergence of

\vec F=x^2\hat i+xy\hat j.

Here

M=x^2, \qquad N=xy.

So

div(\vec F)=M_x+N_y=2x+x=3x.

At (2,1),

div(\vec F)=6.

Divergence measures local source strength and appears in the flux form of Green’s theorem.

Divergence Theorem in the Plane

Convert outward flux across a closed curve into a double integral of divergence over the enclosed region.

Let S be the unit disk. Use the divergence theorem in the plane to compute the outward flux of

\vec F=x\hat i+y\hat j

across its boundary \partial S.

Since

div(\vec F)=1+1=2,

The divergence theorem in the plane gives

\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\hat n\mathrm ds =\iint_S div(\vec F)\mathrm dA =\iint_S 2\mathrm dA

Since S is the unit disk,

\iint_S 2\mathrm dA=2\pi

The divergence theorem in the plane uses divergence; Green’s theorem for circulation uses curl.

Integrals in 3D Space

Triple Integrals

Set the bounds by describing the solid one coordinate at a time.

Let

E=\{(x,y,z):x\geq0,\ y\geq0,\ z\geq0,\ x+y+z\leq1\}

Find the volume and center of mass of E with uniform density.

Figure 1 shows the first-octant tetrahedron and its triangular projection onto the xy-plane.

The solid lies below z=1-x-y over the triangle 0\leq x\leq1, 0\leq y\leq1-x. Thus

\begin{aligned} V &=\iiint_E1\,\mathrm dV \\ &=\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}\int_0^{1-x-y} 1\,\mathrm dz\,\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dx \\ &=\frac16 \end{aligned}

By symmetry, \bar x=\bar y=\bar z. The first moment in the x direction is

\begin{aligned} \iiint_E x\,\mathrm dV &=\frac12\int_0^1x(1-x)^2\,\mathrm dx \\ &=\frac1{24} \end{aligned}

Therefore,

\vec r_{\mathrm{cm}} =\left\langle\frac14,\frac14,\frac14\right\rangle

Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

Choose coordinates that match the symmetry, and include the Jacobian in \mathrm dV.

Cylindrical coordinates

Find the volume enclosed by the paraboloid z=x^2+y^2 and the plane z=4.

In cylindrical coordinates, z=r^2, and the surfaces meet at r=2:

\begin{aligned} V &=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_{r^2}^4 r\,\mathrm dz\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta \\ &=2\pi\int_0^2(4-r^2)r\,\mathrm dr \\ &=8\pi \end{aligned}

Spherical coordinates

Let B_a be the ball x^2+y^2+z^2\leq a^2. Evaluate

\iiint_{B_a}(x^2+y^2+z^2)\,\mathrm dV

Since x^2+y^2+z^2=\rho^2,

\begin{aligned} \iiint_{B_a}\rho^2\,\mathrm dV &=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^a \rho^4\sin\phi\,\mathrm d\rho\,\mathrm d\phi\,\mathrm d\theta \\ &=\frac{4\pi a^5}{5} \end{aligned}

Vector Fields in 3D Space

Evaluate a vector field componentwise before taking its magnitude or a directional component.

For

\vec F(x,y,z)=yz\,\hat i+xz\,\hat j+xy\,\hat k

find \vec F(1,-1,2), its magnitude, and its component along \hat u=\langle2,-1,2\rangle/3.

Substitution gives

\vec F(1,-1,2)=\langle-2,2,-1\rangle, \qquad |\vec F(1,-1,2)|=3

The component along \hat u is

\vec F(1,-1,2)\cdot\hat u =\frac{-4-2-2}{3} =-\frac83

Surface Integrals and Flux

Choose an oriented vector area element before computing flux. Use \mathrm dS instead when only scalar surface area is needed.

Graph surface

Let S be the part of z=1-x-y above

D=\{(x,y):x\geq0,\ y\geq0,\ x+y\leq1\}

with upward orientation. Find the flux of \vec F=\langle x,y,z\rangle through S.

For f(x,y)=1-x-y,

\mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{up}} =\langle-f_x,-f_y,1\rangle\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy =\langle1,1,1\rangle\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy

On the surface,

\vec F(x,y,f(x,y))\cdot\langle1,1,1\rangle =x+y+(1-x-y)=1

Therefore,

\iint_S\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec S =\iint_D1\,\mathrm dA =\frac12

Implicit surface

Let S be the upper hemisphere x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2. Use its projection onto the xy-plane to find its area and the upward flux of \hat k.

Set w=x^2+y^2+z^2. On S,

\nabla w=\langle2x,2y,2z\rangle, \qquad w_z=2z, \qquad z=\sqrt{a^2-r^2}

Hence

\mathrm dS =\frac{|\nabla w|}{|w_z|}\,\mathrm dA =\frac{a}{z}\,\mathrm dA

and

\mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{up}} =\frac{\nabla w}{w_z}\,\mathrm dA =\left\langle\frac{x}{z},\frac{y}{z},1\right\rangle\mathrm dA

The surface area is

\begin{aligned} \iint_S1\,\mathrm dS &=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2-r^2}}r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta \\ &=2\pi a^2 \end{aligned}

The upward flux of \hat k is the area of the projected disk:

\iint_S\hat k\cdot\mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{up}} =\iint_{x^2+y^2\leq a^2}1\,\mathrm dA =\pi a^2

Divergence in 3D Space

Compute divergence by differentiating each component in its matching coordinate.

For

\vec F=x^2\hat i+xy\hat j+xz\hat k

find \operatorname{div}\vec F and interpret its value at \mathbf p=(1,-2,3).

\operatorname{div}\vec F =\frac{\partial x^2}{\partial x} +\frac{\partial(xy)}{\partial y} +\frac{\partial(xz)}{\partial z} =4x

Thus

\operatorname{div}\vec F(\mathbf p)=4

For a small volume \Delta V around \mathbf p, the outward flux is approximately 4\Delta V.

Divergence Theorem

Use the divergence theorem when a volume integral is easier than direct flux through every piece of a closed surface.

Closed-surface flux

Let E be the ball x^2+y^2+z^2\leq a^2, and let

\vec F=x\hat i+y\hat j+z\hat k

Find the outward flux through \partial E.

Since \operatorname{div}\vec F=3,

\begin{aligned} \iint_{\partial E}\vec F\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dS &=\iiint_E3\,\mathrm dV \\ &=3\left(\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}\right) \\ &=4\pi a^3 \end{aligned}

As a direct check, \vec F=a\hat n on \partial E, so

\iint_{\partial E}\vec F\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dS =a(4\pi a^2) =4\pi a^3

Diffusion Equation

Derive the diffusion equation from conservation, then verify a fundamental solution.

Let u(x,y,z,t) be a concentration and \vec J its flux density. For a diffusion coefficient k>0, Fick’s law is

\vec J=-k\nabla u

With no internal sources, conservation in every fixed solid E gives

\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\iiint_Eu\,\mathrm dV =-\iint_{\partial E}\vec J\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dS

Apply the divergence theorem and move the time derivative inside the integral:

\iiint_E \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\vec J\right)\mathrm dV =0

Since this holds for every E,

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} =-\nabla\cdot\vec J =\nabla\cdot(k\nabla u)

For constant k, this reduces to

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=k\nabla^2u

Verify a proposed solution by comparing its time derivative with its Laplacian.

For k>0 and t>0, let

u(x,y,z,t) =A t^{-3/2} \exp\left(-\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{4kt}\right)

Verify that u satisfies u_t=k\nabla^2u.

Write r^2=x^2+y^2+z^2. Differentiating with respect to time gives

u_t =\left(-\frac{3}{2t}+\frac{r^2}{4kt^2}\right)u

For each coordinate x_i\in\{x,y,z\},

\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i} =-\frac{x_i}{2kt}u, \qquad \frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x_i^2} =\left(-\frac{1}{2kt}+\frac{x_i^2}{4k^2t^2}\right)u

Therefore,

\begin{aligned} k\nabla^2u &=k\left(-\frac{3}{2kt}+\frac{r^2}{4k^2t^2}\right)u \\ &=\left(-\frac{3}{2t}+\frac{r^2}{4kt^2}\right)u \\ &=u_t \end{aligned}

The factor t^{-3/2} keeps \iiint_{\mathbb R^3}u\,\mathrm dV independent of t while the Gaussian width grows like \sqrt t.

Curl in 3D Space

Compute curl componentwise, then interpret its component along a chosen normal direction.

For

\vec F=-y\hat i+x\hat j+z\hat k

find \nabla\times\vec F and the local circulation density in a plane normal to \hat k.

With P=-y, Q=x, and R=z,

\begin{aligned} \nabla\times\vec F &=(R_y-Q_z)\hat i+(P_z-R_x)\hat j+(Q_x-P_y)\hat k \\ &=2\hat k \end{aligned}

Thus

(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\hat k=2

For a small positively oriented loop enclosing area \Delta A in a plane normal to \hat k, the circulation is approximately 2\Delta A.

Stokes’ Theorem

Use any convenient oriented surface with the required boundary.

Let S be the outward-oriented upper hemisphere x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2, and let

\vec F=-\frac y2\hat i+\frac x2\hat j

Find the circulation around the positively oriented equator \partial S. For the outward orientation of the upper hemisphere, \partial S is counterclockwise when viewed from the positive z-axis.

Since

\nabla\times\vec F=\hat k

replace the hemisphere by the upward-oriented disk D=\{(x,y,0):x^2+y^2\leq a^2\}, which has the same oriented boundary:

\begin{aligned} \oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r &=\iint_D(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\hat k\,\mathrm dA \\ &=\iint_D1\,\mathrm dA \\ &=\pi a^2 \end{aligned}

Direct parametrization gives the same result. For \vec r(t)=\langle a\cos t,a\sin t,0\rangle, 0\leq t\leq2\pi,

\vec F(\vec r(t))\cdot\vec r'(t)=\frac{a^2}{2}

and hence

\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r =\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{a^2}{2}\,\mathrm dt =\pi a^2
