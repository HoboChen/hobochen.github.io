Calculus II (18.02)

Vectors and Matrices

Product

\begin{aligned} \vec A \cdot \vec B & = \sum a_ib_i = |\vec A||\vec B| \cos \theta \\ \vec A \times \vec B & = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i} \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & a_3 \\ b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} - \hat{j} \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} + \hat{k} \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ b_1 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} \\ |\vec A \times \vec B| & = \text{area of space parallelogram} = |\vec A||\vec B|\sin \theta\\ \frac{\vec A \times \vec B}{|\vec A \times \vec B|} &= \hat{n}\text{ perpendicular to plane formed by } \vec A \text{ and } \vec B \\ \end{aligned}

The direction of \vec A \times \vec B is determined by the right-hand rule: point the fingers of your right hand in the direction of \vec A, curl them toward \vec B, and your thumb points in the direction of \vec A \times \vec B.

\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\bigl(\vec{A} \times \vec{B}\bigr) = \frac{\mathrm d\vec{A}}{\mathrm dt} \times \vec{B} \;+\; \vec{A} \times \frac{\mathrm d\vec{B}}{\mathrm dt}

Determinant

\begin{aligned} |\det(\vec A, \vec B)| & = \text {area of parallelogram} \\ |\det(\vec A, \vec B, \vec C)| & = |\vec A \cdot (\vec B \times \vec C)| = \text {volume of parallelepiped} \end{aligned}

Inverse Matrix

\mathbf{A}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det({\mathbf{A}})}\text{adj}(\mathbf{A})

Partial Derivatives

Partial Derivatives

To treat all other variables as constant:

f_x = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0+\Delta x, y_0) - f(x_0, y_0)}{\Delta x}

Least Square

To find the best-fit line y = ax + b for x_i, y_i:

\text{min } [ D_{a, b} = \sum_i (y_i - (ax_i + b)) ^ 2 ] \iff \frac{\partial D}{\partial a} = \frac {\partial D}{\partial b} = 0

Min/Max

Look at second derivatives, f_{xx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}, f_{xy}, f_{yx}, f_{yy}, fact(Clairaut’s theorem): f_{xy} = f_{yx}.

Given f and critical point (x_0, y_0), let A = f_{xx}(x_0, y_0), B = f_{xy}(x_0, y_0), C = f_{yy}(x_0, y_0), then:

  • AC - B^2 > 0: A>0 local min, A<0 local max
  • AC - B^2 < 0: saddle
  • AC - B^2 = 0: inconclusive, see higher derivative

Taylor’s Theorem

For f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, which is k-times continuously differentiable at \mathbf{x_0} \in \mathbb{R}^n, then there exists function h_\alpha:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}, where |\alpha| = k, such that:

f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{|\alpha| \leq k} \frac {D^\alpha f(\mathbf x_0)}{\alpha!}(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x_0})^\alpha + \sum_{|\alpha| = k}h_\alpha(\mathbf x)(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x_0})^\alpha \\ \text{ and } \lim_{\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x_0}}h_\alpha(\mathbf{x}) = 0

Total Differential

For f = f(x, y, z):

\mathrm d f = f_x \mathrm d x + f_y \mathrm d y + f_z \mathrm d z

Chain Rule

For f = f(x, y, z), x = x(t), y = y(t), z = z(t):

\frac{\mathrm d f}{\mathrm d t} = f_x \frac{\mathrm d x}{\mathrm d t} + f_y \frac{\mathrm d y}{\mathrm d t} + f_z \frac{\mathrm d z}{\mathrm d t}

For w = f(x, y), x = x(u, v), y = y(u, v):

\begin{aligned} \mathrm d w & = f_x \mathrm d x + f_y \mathrm d y = f_x (x_u \mathrm d u + x_v \mathrm d v) + f_y (y_u \mathrm d u + y_v \mathrm d v) \\ \implies \frac{\partial f}{\partial u} & = f_x x_u + f_y y_u = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u} \\ \end{aligned}

Gradient Vector

For w = w(x, y, z), x = x(t), y = y(t), z = z(t), to be perpendicular to the level surfaces w = c:

\nabla w = \langle w_x, w_y, w_z \rangle

Combine with chain rule, given \vec r(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle, w = w(\vec r(t)):

\begin{aligned} \frac {\mathrm d} {\mathrm d t} w(\vec r(t)) & = \langle w_x(\vec r(t)), w_y(\vec r (t)), w_z(\vec r (t)) \rangle \cdot \frac {\mathrm d \vec r} {\mathrm d t} \\ & = \nabla w(\vec r(t)) \cdot \vec r'(t) \end{aligned} \qquad{(1)}

Rate of Change in an Arbitrary Direction

For w as move in some direction:

\frac{\mathrm d w}{\mathrm d s \left.\right| \hat{u}} = \nabla w \cdot \hat{u} = \left|\nabla w\right|\cos \theta

Lagrange Multipliers

To get min/max of f = f(x, y, z) given g(x, y, z) = c:

\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g

Partial Derivative with Non-Independent Variables

If f = f(x, y, z), g(x, y, z) = c, ask for (\frac{\partial f}{\partial z})_{y}:

\begin{aligned} & \mathrm d g = 0 \to g_x\mathrm d x + g_y\mathrm d y + g_z \mathrm d z = 0 \\ & \mathrm d y = 0 \to g_x\mathrm d x + g_z \mathrm d z = 0 \iff dx = -\frac{g_z \mathrm d z}{g_x} \\ & \begin{aligned} \mathrm d f &= f_x\mathrm d x + f_y\mathrm d y + f_z \mathrm d z \\ & = f_x\mathrm d x + f_z \mathrm d z \\ & = f_x(-\frac{g_z \mathrm d z}{g_x}) + f_z \mathrm d z \\ & = \frac{f_zg_x-f_xg_z}{g_x}\mathrm d z \end{aligned} \\ & \implies (\frac{\partial f}{\partial z})_{y} = \frac{g_xf_z-f_xg_z}{g_x} \end{aligned}

Double Integral in Plane

Double Integral

For the volume below z = f(x, y) over plane region R=\{(x,y): a\le x\le b,\ g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\}:

\iint _{R}f(x, y)\mathrm d A = \int_a^b\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x,y)\mathrm dy \mathrm dx

Polar Coordinates

\mathrm d A = r\mathrm d r \mathrm d \theta

Area of Region

\iint_R1\mathrm d A

Center of Mass

With density function \delta(x, y):

\begin{aligned} \overline x = \frac{1}{\iint_R \delta \mathrm d A} \iint_R x\delta \mathrm d A \\ \overline y = \frac{1}{\iint_R \delta \mathrm d A} \iint_R y\delta \mathrm d A \end{aligned}

Rotational Moment of Inertia

I = \iint_R (\text{distance to axis})^2\delta(x, y) \mathrm d A

Jacobian Matrix

For u=u(x, y), v = v(x, y):

\mathbf{J} = \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}= \begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix}

More generally for \mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^m: \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbf{f(x)} \in \mathbb{R}^m:

\mathbf{J}_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}

Change of Variables

For u=u(x, y), v = v(x, y):

\mathrm d u \mathrm d v = \left|\mathbf{J}\right| \mathrm d x \mathrm d y

Line Integral in Plane

Vector Fields

\vec{F} = M \hat{i} + N \hat{j}, \quad M = M(x,y), N = N(x, y)

Line Integral

For work done by a force field, work is the dot product of force and displacement, d\vec r = \mathrm d x \hat i + \mathrm d y \hat j:

\begin{aligned} W &= \lim_{|\Delta \vec r_i|\to 0} \sum_i \vec F \cdot \Delta \vec r_i \\ &= \int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r \\ &= \int_C (Mdx + Ndy ) \end{aligned}

Equivalently, since \mathrm d\vec r = \hat T\ \mathrm ds, where \hat T is the unit tangent vector to the curve:

W = \int_C \vec F \cdot \hat T \mathrm ds

Gradient Field

If there exists a function that \vec F = \nabla f, then \vec F is called gradient field; f is called potential function.

Given C = \vec r(t), t \in [a,b]:

\int_C \vec F \cdot \mathrm d \vec r = \int_C \nabla f \cdot \mathrm d \vec r \overset{\href{#eq:gradient-chain-rule}{\text{(1)}}}{=} \int_a^b \nabla f(\vec r(t)) \cdot \vec r'(t) \mathrm d t = \int_a^b \frac {\mathrm d} {\mathrm d t} f(\vec r(t)) \mathrm d t = f(\vec r(b)) - f(\vec r(a))

N_x = M_y \overset{*}{\iff} \vec F \text{ is a gradient field } \iff \vec F \text{ is conservative: } \oint_C \vec F \cdot \mathrm d \vec r = 0 \text{ for any closed curve}

* holds if \vec F\in C^1(S) and S is simply connected.

Simply-connected region is a plane region S if every closed curve in S encloses only points in S.

Curl

Curl measures the limiting circulation per unit area near a point.

The circulation of a vector field \vec F around the positively oriented (counterclockwise) boundary \partial S of a plane region S is

\Gamma_{\partial S}=\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r

For a planar vector field \vec F=M(x,y)\hat i+N(x,y)\hat j,

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{curl}\vec F(\mathbf p) &=\lim_{S\to\mathbf p} \frac{1}{\operatorname{area}(S)} \oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r \\ &=N_x(\mathbf p)-M_y(\mathbf p) \end{aligned}

Here, S shrinks to \mathbf p and \partial S is positively oriented. Positive curl corresponds to counterclockwise local circulation.

For a planar field, this scalar curl is the \hat k component of the three-dimensional curl.

Green’s Theorem

For a plane region S with positively oriented boundary \partial S, the circulation around \partial S equals the double integral of scalar curl over S.

\begin{aligned} \oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r =\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\,\mathrm dA \iff{}& \oint_{\partial S}M\,\mathrm dx+N\,\mathrm dy =\iint_S(N_x-M_y)\,\mathrm dA \\ \iff{}& \oint_{\partial S}M\,\mathrm dx =\iint_S-M_y\,\mathrm dA \land \oint_{\partial S}N\,\mathrm dy =\iint_SN_x\,\mathrm dA \end{aligned}

Flux

For a plane region S with positively oriented boundary \partial S, the outward flux is

\begin{aligned} \Phi &= \lim_{\Delta {s_i}\to0}\sum_i{(\vec F \cdot \hat{n}) \Delta {s_i}} \\ &= \oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm ds \\ &=\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\langle\mathrm dy,-\mathrm dx\rangle \\ &=\oint_{\partial S}-N\,\mathrm dx+M\,\mathrm dy \end{aligned}

Divergence

Divergence measures the limiting outward flux per unit area:

\operatorname{div}\vec F=M_x+N_y

Divergence Theorem in the Plane

Let S be a plane region with positively oriented, piecewise smooth boundary \partial S. If \vec F\in C^1 on an open set containing S, then

\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm ds =\iint_S\operatorname{div}\vec F\,\mathrm dA \iff \oint_{\partial S}-N\,\mathrm dx+M\,\mathrm dy =\iint_S(M_x+N_y)\,\mathrm dA

This is Green’s theorem in flux form.

Integrals in 3D Space

Triple Integrals

For a scalar field f over a solid region E,

\iiint_E f\,\mathrm dV

If E=\{(x,y,z):(x,y)\in D,\ g_1(x,y)\le z\le g_2(x,y)\}, then

\iiint_E f\,\mathrm dV =\iint_D\int_{g_1(x,y)}^{g_2(x,y)}f(x,y,z)\,\mathrm dz\,\mathrm dA

Setting f=1 gives volume. For density \delta(x,y,z) and position vector \vec r=\langle x,y,z\rangle,

m=\iiint_E\delta\,\mathrm dV, \qquad \vec r_{\mathrm{cm}} =\frac{1}{m}\iiint_E\vec r\,\delta\,\mathrm dV

Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates (r,\theta,z) satisfy

\begin{aligned} x&=r\cos\theta \\ y&=r\sin\theta \end{aligned}

Here r\ge0 and 0\le\theta<2\pi. The Jacobian gives

\mathrm dV=r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm dz

Spherical coordinates (\rho,\phi,\theta) satisfy

\begin{aligned} x&=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta \\ y&=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta \\ z&=\rho\cos\phi \\ r&=\rho\sin\phi \end{aligned}

Here \rho\ge0, 0\le\phi\le\pi, and 0\le\theta<2\pi.

Figure 1 defines the radial distance \rho, polar angle \phi, azimuthal angle \theta, and cylindrical radius r.

The spherical-coordinate Jacobian gives

\mathrm dV =\rho^2\sin\phi\,\mathrm d\rho\,\mathrm d\phi\,\mathrm d\theta

On the sphere \rho=a, the scalar and outward-oriented surface elements are

\begin{aligned} \mathrm dS &=a^2\sin\phi\,\mathrm d\phi\,\mathrm d\theta \\ \mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{out}} &=\hat e_\rho\,a^2\sin\phi\,\mathrm d\phi\,\mathrm d\theta \end{aligned}

Vector Fields in 3D Space

A vector field in three-dimensional space has the form

\vec F =P(x,y,z)\hat i+Q(x,y,z)\hat j+R(x,y,z)\hat k

Surface Integrals and Flux

Let an oriented smooth surface S be parametrized by \vec r=\vec r(u,v) over a region D. If \vec r_u\times\vec r_v\ne\vec0, then

\begin{aligned} \mathrm dS &=|\vec r_u\times\vec r_v|\,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv \\ \mathrm d\vec S &=(\vec r_u\times\vec r_v)\,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv \end{aligned}

The order of u,v determines the orientation. Reversing the order reverses \mathrm d\vec S. The flux of \vec F through S is

\begin{aligned} \Phi &=\iint_S\vec F\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dS =\iint_S\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec S \\ &=\iint_D \vec F(\vec r(u,v))\cdot (\vec r_u\times\vec r_v)\,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv \end{aligned}

For a graph z=f(x,y) with upward orientation,

\begin{aligned} \mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{up}} &=\langle-f_x,-f_y,1\rangle\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy \\ \mathrm dS &=\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy \end{aligned}

The downward-oriented area element is -\mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{up}}.

For an implicit surface w(x,y,z)=c with w_z\ne0, projected onto the xy-plane, let \vec N=\nabla w. A unit normal is

\hat n=\pm\frac{\vec N}{|\vec N|}

If \alpha is the angle between \hat n and \hat k, the projected area element satisfies

\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy =|\cos\alpha|\,\mathrm dS =|\hat n\cdot\hat k|\,\mathrm dS

Therefore,

\begin{aligned} \mathrm dS &=\frac{1}{|\hat n\cdot\hat k|}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy \\ &=\frac{|\nabla w|}{|\nabla w\cdot\hat k|}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy \\ &=\frac{|\nabla w|}{|w_z|}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy \end{aligned}

For the upward orientation,

\begin{aligned} \hat n_{\mathrm{up}} &=\frac{w_z}{|w_z|}\frac{\nabla w}{|\nabla w|} \\ \mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{up}} &=\hat n_{\mathrm{up}}\,\mathrm dS =\frac{\nabla w}{w_z}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy \end{aligned}

The downward-oriented vector area element is -\mathrm d\vec S_{\mathrm{up}}. Projection onto the yz- or xz-plane uses w_x or w_y respectively.

Divergence in 3D Space

Divergence measures the limiting outward flux per unit volume:

\operatorname{div}\vec F =\nabla\cdot\vec F =P_x+Q_y+R_z

Divergence Theorem

Let E be a bounded solid whose piecewise smooth boundary \partial E is oriented outward. If \vec F\in C^1 on an open set containing \overline E, then

\iint_{\partial E}\vec F\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dS =\iiint_E\nabla\cdot\vec F\,\mathrm dV

This is the three-dimensional analogue of the divergence theorem in the plane.

Curl in 3D Space

The component (\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\hat n measures the limiting circulation per unit area in a plane normal to \hat n:

\operatorname{curl}\vec F =\nabla\times\vec F = \begin{vmatrix} \hat i & \hat j & \hat k \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ P & Q & R \end{vmatrix} =(R_y-Q_z)\hat i+(P_z-R_x)\hat j+(Q_x-P_y)\hat k

For a planar field \vec F=M(x,y)\hat i+N(x,y)\hat j, viewed as a three-dimensional field with zero \hat k component, this reduces to

\nabla\times\vec F=(N_x-M_y)\hat k

Stokes’ Theorem

Let S be an oriented piecewise smooth surface whose boundary \partial S is positively oriented, so that \partial S and the chosen normal \hat n obey the right-hand rule. If \vec F\in C^1 on an open set containing S, then

\oint_{\partial S}\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec r =\iint_S(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dS

This is the three-dimensional analogue of Green’s theorem for circulation.
